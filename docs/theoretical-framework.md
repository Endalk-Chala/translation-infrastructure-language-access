# Theoretical Framework — Working Notes

## Central proposition

AI translation should be studied not only as a model-performance problem but as a form of **communicative infrastructure**.

Global platform companies make consequential decisions about which languages receive model support, which products expose that support, how systems are documented, how translation quality is maintained, and what forms of institutional communication become practically accessible through AI.

## Working theoretical bridge

The project currently brings together four strands:

1. **Language access**  
   Access is not achieved merely because text can be translated. Meaningful access depends on whether people can understand rights, obligations, eligibility, deadlines, warnings, and next steps.

2. **Communicative infrastructure**  
   Translation tools operate as infrastructure when they become routine intermediaries between institutions and language communities.

3. **Infrastructural inequality**  
   Language support is unevenly distributed. Differences in model coverage, training resources, product integration, and institutional investment may create unequal communicative capacities across languages.

4. **Intercultural and institutional communication**  
   Translation quality includes pragmatic and cultural meaning as well as literal semantics. Institutional communication is especially consequential because small shifts in modality, timing, eligibility, or terminology can alter what a user believes they may, must, or should do.

## Working claim

A translation can be fluent and semantically plausible while still failing as language access if it changes the practical meaning of a right, deadline, eligibility rule, institutional term, warning, or required action.

## Empirical implication

The Google–Meta comparison is therefore evidence about how translation infrastructure performs in practice, not simply a leaderboard of model quality.
